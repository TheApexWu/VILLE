# WHY VILLE

Why this object should exist. Read after `NOT.md`.

---

Most projects that describe cities are made by states, platforms, or the people they pay. Cadastral maps describe what can be taxed. Google Maps describes what can be queried, routed, and monetized. Smart-city dashboards describe what can be optimized. Each is a *legibility instrument* in James Scott's sense — a representation that exists because someone with power needed to *see like a state*, and what doesn't fit the schema disappears.

The result is that the dominant memory of cities is not made by the people who live in them. It is made for them, for purposes that are not theirs, in formats they cannot edit, on infrastructure they cannot own. Harley showed that maps lie by selection. Wood showed that maps make claims and pass them off as fact. Pickles showed that "cartographic reason" is a specifically modern, specifically colonial inheritance. None of this stopped. Every "smart city" deployment is the same operation in a new container.

VILLE exists because a city is not a problem to be solved. It is an inheritance to be argued over.

The inheritance is layered and partly destroyed. Paris had a Roman forum at Lutèce; nobody alive remembers it; what we have is INRAP excavation reports, surviving stones, period scholarship, and the absence of everything else. Paris had a medieval Île de la Cité; Haussmann demolished most of it in the 1860s for political reasons (insurrection control, real-estate speculation) that the official record systematically understated. Marville photographed the streets a few months before the wrecking crews arrived. Atget photographed what survived sixty years later. Each layer is partial, dated, signed by someone with motives, and most of it is gone.

A serious response to this loss has three obligations. First, to *reconstruct* what can honestly be reconstructed — using the best methods available, including build-time machine learning, with the discipline that the synthesis tier of every element is declared in the artifact itself. Second, to *refuse to flatten what cannot* — opacity is structural in the format; absence has a taxonomy; disputed values are held, not resolved. Third, to *hand the result to people in a form they can fork, own, and walk through* — open file format, plain files, CC0 spec, web viewer, no cloud, no account, no platform.

This is not Google Maps. It is not a smart-city product. It is not a video game. It is not a startup. It is closer to a public-domain encyclopedia with rendered geometry: an open, layered, provenance-stamped archive of one city, made slowly, by one person, with the methods of his time, in the open, until he stops or someone else continues.

The phone fails in three places where cities live: underground, in surveillance-aware contexts, and in *deep place* — the relation Tuan called *topophilia* and Augé warned was being replaced by *non-place*. Most contemporary urban tech manufactures non-place at scale. VILLE's job is the opposite: to re-inscribe anthropological place into an artifact that respects the right not to be reduced.

The deeper claim — the one that makes this worth a decade of weekends — is that **memory infrastructure should be slow enough to outlive the people who built it, small enough to be carried, and plural enough to disagree with itself**. The format is the artwork. The web viewer is the demonstration. The essays are the body of work. The device, if it ever exists, is downstream of all of these.

If the project ever drifts toward growth, virality, recurring revenue, or a cap table, the answer is in NOT.md. The discipline is the work.
